## Frameworks

### 12 Levers for Growing Initial Marketplace Supply (How to Kickstart and Scale a Marketplace Business – Part 3: Cracking the Chicken-and-Egg Problem 🐣 - Growing Initial Supply)
A comprehensive ranked framework of the 12 supply-growth strategies used by 17 of today's biggest marketplace companies, sorted by frequency of use. Used to identify which levers to prioritize for your marketplace.

How it works: Levers ranked by frequency of use:
1. Direct sales (~60% of companies) — Door-to-door, cold calling, in-person demos, field sales
2. Referrals (~33%) — Incentivizing existing supply to refer new supply, ambassador programs
3. Piggy-back off existing network (~33%) — Mostly recruiting from Craigslist
4. Word of mouth (~25%) — Organic spread through tight industry networks
5. Subsidizing (~25%) — Income guarantees, free equipment, subsidized leads
6. Employees are the early supply — Company employees act as supply to create liquidity
7. Single-player mode — Offer supply a standalone tool valuable without demand side
8. Performance marketing — SEM, display, Facebook ads (rarely used for supply)
9. Loops/Flywheels — Demand converts to supply, free converts to paid
10. Events — Meetups, onboarding classes, in-person events
11. SEO/Content marketing — Blog posts, white papers, UGC/SEO loops
12. Leverage your community — Street teams, local chapters of passionate members

Key meta-insight: Median number of levers per company = 2 (average = 2.5). Focus on finding your 1-2 working levers and double down.

### 4 Primary Supply Growth Channels for Labor Marketplaces (Kickstarting supply in a labor marketplace)
A prioritized framework of the four most common channels used by successful labor marketplaces to acquire initial supply, based on research of ~12 companies

How it works: The four primary channels, ranked by popularity:

1. **Job Boards** — Post generic roles to existing job boards (Indeed, ZipRecruiter, Craigslist, SimplyHired). Most popular channel, especially for companies focused on one type of role. Can provide ~50% of early supply. Key insight: go where your customers already look for work.

2. **Targeted Direct Outreach** — Cold outreach to strangers, leveraging personal networks, scraping LinkedIn/GitHub for lists, in-person recruitment at workplaces. Second most common. Key insight: do things that don't scale.

3. **Word of Mouth + Paid Referrals** — Organic word of mouth from great product experience, amplified by cash referral programs. Hard to engineer without product-market fit. Especially powerful in B2B labor marketplaces where workers interact with each other. Key insight: build something so great supply can't help but tell friends.

4. **Paid Social** — Ads on LinkedIn (Sponsored Updates, Text Ads), Facebook (custom audiences), Google. LinkedIn was the top performer for professional marketplaces. Key insight: experiment with LinkedIn ads.

### Airbnb's Three-Stage Supply-Demand Diagnosis Evolution (How To Know If You're Supply or Demand Constrained 🤹‍♂️ - Phase 2 of Kickstarting and Scaling a Marketplace Business)
A progression model showing how Airbnb evolved its approach to diagnosing supply vs. demand constraint from simple to sophisticated

How it works: Stage 1: Simple occupancy rate threshold — if occupancy rate was above a certain percentage in a market, that market was supply constrained. Stage 2: Occupancy rate vs. bookings rate model — looked at the relationship between occupancy rate and bookings rate, and identified the downward inflection point to determine at what occupancy rate a market became supply constrained. Stage 3: Econometrics model — for each market, calculates whether you get more incremental revenue from one additional unit of supply or one additional unit of demand.

### DoorDash's Three Supply-Side Growth Levers (How To Know If You're Supply or Demand Constrained 🤹‍♂️ - Phase 2 of Kickstarting and Scaling a Marketplace Business)
The three supply-oriented factors DoorDash always focused on to drive marketplace growth

How it works: When DoorDash looked at growth, it was always one of three things: 1. Selection — the breadth of restaurant and item choices available, 2. Delivery quality — speed, reliability, and accuracy of deliveries, 3. Affordability — pricing and value for customers. All three are supply-oriented, which is why DoorDash was essentially always supply constrained.

### Guarantees vs. Bonuses for Supply-Side Retention (What They'd Do Differently 🔮 Kickstarting and Scaling a Marketplace Business)
A framework for designing supply-side incentives that are habit-forming rather than transactional

How it works: Problem: Juicy one-time bonuses (e.g., 'Join now, get $1000!') are not habit-forming and don't attract high-intent users. Solution: Guarantees that anchor supply in reality. Example: 'If you drive 20 hours a week, you are guaranteed to make $2000/week.' Benefits: (1) Anchors the driver in reality; (2) Sets people up for success; (3) Naturally attracts higher-intent users through the funnel; (4) Helps people understand how the platform fits into their lifestyle; (5) Is habit-forming rather than one-time.

### Lyft's Tiered Ambassador/Referral Program Structure (How to Kickstart and Scale a Marketplace Business – Part 3: Cracking the Chicken-and-Egg Problem 🐣 - Growing Initial Supply)
A multi-tiered referral and ambassador program design targeting students, combining online referrals with on-the-ground ambassador programs for city launches

How it works: Two-pronged approach:

1. Online Referral Program:
- More impactful for top-line supply growth
- Incentivized existing drivers to refer new drivers

2. On-the-Ground Ambassador Program:
- Key piece of city launch strategy
- Targeted students (matched primary younger/millennial audience)
- Seeded supply AND demand ahead of launch
- Reduced overhead when launching a new city
- Tiered reward structure:
  - Lower tiers: monetary incentives (some students made enough for weekly lobster dinners / six-figure incomes)
  - Top tier: essentially a part-time job level of engagement
  - Top tier reward: Letter of recommendation from the COO of Lyft (highly valued by students, very cheap for the company)

Key insight: Ambassador programs were essential for the launch strategy in each city — they got the word out early and seeded both supply and demand before official launch.

### Marketplace City Expansion Timing Framework (Marketplace city expansion strategy)
A decision framework for when to expand a marketplace into a second market, based on signals from 6 major marketplaces

How it works: Expand when these conditions are met:
1. First market is 'working' — flywheel has started (customers return, supply sticks, transactions happen without hand-holding)
2. You have a semblance of a repeatable playbook
3. You have the resources (people, capital) to support a second market
4. Exception: In highly competitive markets with VC funding, expand more aggressively even before all conditions are fully met

PMF milestone examples:
- Caviar: 100 average daily orders
- Snackpass: Over 90% of new users from word of mouth, high engagement (orders/week) and retention
- Instacart: 'Nailed operations' and built tools to support multi-city
- Grubhub: Had a playbook to make a market successful and resources weren't fully absorbed in first city

Typical timeline: 6-12 months post-launch

### Marketplace Cold-Start Supply Strategy (The inside story of Facebook Marketplace)
How to seed supply in a new marketplace by leveraging existing user behavior rather than recruiting new sellers

How it works: Step 1: Identify where users are already buying/selling organically on your platform. Step 2: Create opt-in mechanisms for communities to self-identify as commerce-oriented. Step 3: Structure organic content as proper listings (price, photos, descriptions). Step 4: Build a frictionless cross-posting tool ('one-checkbox experience') so sellers can push existing listings to the new marketplace surface without creating new content. Step 5: Activate cross-posting 2 months before marketplace launch in each region to assess quality and diversity of inventory. Step 6: Monitor buyer engagement metrics — if weekly active buyer retention is healthy, expand to the next region.

### Marketplace Constraint Decision Tree (How to kickstart and scale a marketplace business)
A decision framework for how to constrain your initial marketplace based on whether your service is location-dependent or not.

How it works: Decision: Does the service require supply and demand to be in the same physical location?

IF YES → Constrain by GEOGRAPHY (single market/city)
  - Examples: Uber, Lyft, Airbnb, DoorDash, Caviar, Postmates, Instacart, OpenTable, Rover, Breather, Zillow
  - Pick a market with favorable characteristics (early adopters, high density, natural demand)
  - Define critical mass thresholds for that market
  - Duplicate what works in exact same way before expanding

IF NO → Constrain by CATEGORY
  - Examples: Etsy (vintage, craft supplies, handmade), TaskRabbit (handyman, cleaning, moving), Eventbrite (tech mixers/conferences)
  - Focus on most popular or highest-traction categories first
  - Build liquidity in core categories before expanding

RARE EXCEPTION: Go broad in both (Thumbtack)
  - Only viable when: category breadth increases usage frequency dramatically AND geographic breadth provides experimental velocity
  - Thumbtack: broad category increased frequency from once every few years to 8-12x/year

### Marketplace Expansion Decision Summary (Marketplace city expansion strategy)
Lenny's four-point summary framework for marketplace city expansion strategy

2. When it's time to expand: As soon as you feel like your first market is working, have a semblance of a playbook, and have the resources to do it

### Marketplace Growth Inflection Priority: Selection > Quality > Affordability (Growth inflections)
DoorDash's framework for prioritizing marketplace improvements, where selection matters most and price matters least in early years

How it works: For marketplace businesses, customer priorities in order of importance:
1. Selection (most important) - breadth and variety of supply
2. Quality - reliability and experience quality
3. Affordability (least important in early years) - price

This means marketplace growth teams should prioritize expanding selection before optimizing on price.

### Marketplace Launch Concentration Strategy (How long it takes to find Product-Market Fit)
The principle that marketplace startups should concentrate on a single city or category before expanding, with Thumbtack as the counter-example.

How it works: Key principle: Every successful marketplace in this dataset started by concentrating on a single city or category.

Examples of focused launches:
- Lyft: Started in SF only
- DoorDash: Started in one city, then validated in city 2
- Rover: Started in Seattle
- Caviar: Single market focus
- Airbnb: Single market focus

Counter-example:
- Thumbtack: Did NOT focus on a single city/category. Result: took ~5 years to find PMF (the longest in the dataset). They spent 2 years on supply, 2 years on demand, and 1 year on revenue model before all three came together.

The pattern for local marketplaces specifically:
1. Launch in one city/category
2. Achieve initial PMF signals (retention, organic growth)
3. Replicate in a second city to validate durability
4. Then raise capital and scale to more markets

### Marketplace Prioritization Principle (Noam Lovinsky)
A guiding principle for resolving conflicts in marketplace product decisions.

How it works: Order of priority: 1. Customers first (demand). 2. Pros second (supply). 3. Platform last (Thumbtack). Rationale: Serving demand best ultimately serves supply best by providing high-quality liquidity.

### Marketplace Supply-Side Value Proposition Framework (How marketplaces win)
A framework for crafting the supply-side value proposition based on whether your supply consists of people or businesses.

How it works: Two supply-side value proposition types:

1. People-as-supply (e.g., Airbnb hosts, Outschool teachers, Cameo celebs, Rover sitters, Uber drivers):
   - Value prop: 'Make life-changing money doing a new type of work that isn't that hard.'
   - Examples of framing: Dog-sit for $50/hour, drive people around for $20/hour, teach kids math for $100/hour.
   - Your job as marketplace: Make this money feel real and achievable.

2. Businesses-as-supply (e.g., DoorDash restaurants, Faire retailers, Thumbtack service providers):
   - Value prop: 'We'll drive (or save you) some percentage additional revenue for your existing business.'
   - Typically means bringing more demand to existing businesses.
   - Your job as marketplace: Be clear about the economics and make the investment for an SMB feel worth it.

### Marketplace Wedge Rationale (Mike Duboe / Greylock) (Picking a wedge)
Four reasons why narrow initial market focus is especially critical for marketplace and network-effects businesses

How it works: Why marketplace founders should start with a narrow market wedge:

1. LIQUIDITY — It's faster to build liquidity in more narrowly defined market segments
2. PLAYBOOK DEVELOPMENT — Building density in one subvertical helps a team more effectively build a playbook before expanding to the next
3. DEEPER PRODUCT — The narrower the initial focus, the more likely one is to build a deeper and differentiated product
4. DEEPER EMPATHY — Narrower market → deeper end user empathy for founders & product teams. Conversely, too much breadth can introduce noise in early user research & product work

Overall: Starting narrow improves one's likelihood to discover & solve a precise pain point.

Red flag: Too broad an initial market is a flag for marketplace businesses.

### Marketplace Whac-a-Mole (Ramesh Johari)
Mental model for understanding that most marketplace changes redistribute attention and inventory, creating a cascading series of winners and losers rather than net positive outcomes

How it works: Pattern: Fix metrics for new supply → directs them to experienced demand side → new supply metrics improve → existing demand side metrics worsen → fix that → new problem emerges. Root cause: Marketplace management is fundamentally about moving attention and inventory around. Key question (from Servaes Tholen, Upwork/Thumbtack CFO): Are the winners you've created more important to your business than the losers you've created?

### Peer-to-Peer Supply Onboarding Engine (Benjamin Lauzier)
A scalable system for onboarding and activating new supply using top-tier existing users.

How it works: Identify top 10-20% of supply (e.g., 4.9+ star drivers). Pay them a flat fee ($35) to conduct visual inspections, document checks, and ride-alongs for new applicants. This provides social proof, bypasses the need for expensive corporate onboarding centers, and increases retention of the mentors.

### Supply-First vs. Demand-First Decision Framework (How to Kickstart and Scale a Marketplace Business – Part 2: Cracking the Chicken-and-Egg Problem 🐣 - Supply vs. Demand)
A decision framework for marketplace founders to determine whether to focus early resources on supply or demand, based on analysis of 17 successful marketplaces.

How it works: Default: Focus on supply first (80% of successful marketplaces did this — 14 of 17). Supply drives its own demand through: (1) Suppliers bring their own audience/customers (Eventbrite: event creators drove traffic; DoorDash: restaurants bring diners; Caviar: restaurant brands attract orders), (2) Suppliers ARE the early demand (Etsy: sellers were also buyers), (3) Supply growth unlocks demand growth organically (Lyft: more drivers → better experience → more riders).

Exceptions — focus on demand first when: (1) You offer super easy, super flexible, meaningful income — making supply acquisition trivial (Rover: dog lovers who work from home, extra $50 is meaningful; TaskRabbit: thousands on waitlist, had to charge application fee to slow supply), OR (2) Your initial supply is essentially publicly available data used in a new way (Zillow: county and tax assessor records → used to attract demand → then converted to real listings).

Special case: If you find supply and demand aren't interdependent, consider whether you're actually a marketplace at all (Patreon: realized they were a platform, not a marketplace).

### The 'Zero Downside' Supply Acquisition Pitch (How to Kickstart and Scale a Marketplace Business – Part 3: Cracking the Chicken-and-Egg Problem 🐣 - Growing Initial Supply)
GrubHub's approach to supply acquisition: systematically identify and remove every objection suppliers have until there is zero risk to signing up

How it works: Framework: Take every excuse supply has for not using your platform and remove it as an obstacle.

GrubHub's resulting pitch elements:
- You only pay when you receive orders (no upfront cost)
- There are no hidden fees (full transparency)
- You can cancel any time (no lock-in)
- Net result: Zero downside to sign up and give it a try

Process:
1. Go door-to-door during supplier downtime (restaurants during non-peak hours)
2. Talk to owners directly
3. List every objection they raise
4. Systematically build solutions/policies that eliminate each objection
5. Iterate the pitch until the answer to 'why not try?' is 'there's no reason not to'

### Three Patterns of Marketplace Supply-Demand Constraint (How To Know If You're Supply or Demand Constrained 🤹‍♂️ - Phase 2 of Kickstarting and Scaling a Marketplace Business)
A classification framework for understanding which of three patterns your marketplace falls into regarding supply vs. demand constraint, based on analysis of ~20+ marketplace companies

How it works: Pattern 1: Always Supply Constrained (~40% of marketplaces). ROI of adding new supply greatly outweighs investing in driving new demand throughout the company's history. Examples: Uber, Lyft, OpenTable, DoorDash. Pattern 2: Always Demand Constrained (very rare, ~3 companies identified). Supply is easy to acquire but changing customer behavior is the bottleneck. Examples: Rover, TaskRabbit, AngelList. Pattern 3: Varying Constraint by Geography/Category (~40% of marketplaces). Different markets or categories have different supply vs. demand imbalances. Companies need models or heuristics to diagnose which side needs focus per market. Examples: GrubHub, Thumbtack, Airbnb, Zillow, Instacart.

### Three Requirements for Demand-to-Supply Conversion (Demand driving supply: The little-understood growth loop behind a surprising number of iconic billion-dollar companies)
The three things potential supply-side converts need in order to switch sides of a marketplace

How it works: Potential users who may convert to supply need to: 1. Know who you are (brand awareness), 2. Know what you do and how you help them — the 'aha' of your value, specifically in context of the supply-side use case (value understanding), 3. Keep you top-of-mind when that need arises (recall/salience). Start with the higher-traffic and/or most contextually relevant parts of your product where your branding and positioning can be improved.

### Uber's Supply Channel Mix and Efficiency Analysis (How to Kickstart and Scale a Marketplace Business – Part 3: Cracking the Chicken-and-Egg Problem 🐣 - Growing Initial Supply)
Uber's breakdown of early supply acquisition channels by volume and quality, showing that referrals produced the best drivers while paid was least efficient

How it works: Uber's early supply (driver) first-trip breakdown:
- Referrals: ~1/3 of first trips — also the BEST quality drivers
- Word of mouth: ~1/3 of first trips
- Paid channels: ~1/3 of first trips — but accounted for HALF of signups, meaning it was the LEAST efficient channel (highest drop-off from signup to first trip)

Key insight: Volume of signups ≠ quality of supply. Referrals produced both significant volume AND the highest quality drivers. Paid channels had the worst conversion from signup to actual participation.

A structured decision template for choosing and executing supply growth channels based on marketplace characteristics

How it works: Step 1: Assess your marketplace type
- Is your supply focused on one type of role? → Job boards are likely your #1 channel
- Is your supply in a B2B context where workers interact? → Word of mouth will be powerful

Step 3: Layer on supplementary tactics as appropriate
- Upskill your supply (if training adds value)
- Pre-load demand (so supply sees opportunities when they arrive)
- Pitch user stories to press
- Do manual onboarding (reduce friction even if it doesn't scale)
- Study competitor messaging via Wayback Machine

Step 4: Concentrate on a select set of geos and roles to establish density before expanding

## Checklists

### 7 Additional Supply Growth Tactics for Labor Marketplaces (Kickstarting supply in a labor marketplace)
Supplementary tactics beyond the four primary channels, each proven by at least one successful labor marketplace

How it works: 1. **Upskill your supply** — Build cohort-based courses or training programs that attract talent and ensure quality (e.g. Shift built career transition programs for veterans that became talent magnets and a unique selling proposition).

2. **First build a community** — Start with a community (meetup, online group) and build the marketplace product on top after the community reaches critical mass (e.g. Tech Ladies started as a meetup 5 years before building the hiring product).

3. **Make certain there is demand** — Pre-load some quality demand before recruiting supply, even if you need to set expectations with the demand side that early traction will be low (e.g. Roo listed hospital shifts first to attract vet interest).

4. **Get press** — Pitch compelling individual user stories to media outlets; stories can get syndicated across niche blogs and outlets, driving thousands of signups (e.g. The Mom Project's Chicago Tribune profile of a mom who left Google).

5. **Do manual onboarding** — Reduce supply-side friction by collecting only partial information upfront and completing profiles via phone calls (e.g. Vettery did 20-minute calls to build out candidate profiles).

6. **Concentrate on a geo** — Focus heavily on a select set of geographies and roles to establish supply/demand density before expanding; this is critical for scaling (e.g. Hired described this as their most important strategic decision).

7. **Get the messaging right** — Study competitor messaging evolution using the Wayback Machine; test messaging on landing pages and Facebook ads informed by what worked for predecessors (e.g. Incredible Health studied every known marketplace's messaging history).

### Grubhub's 9-Step City Launch Playbook (Marketplace city expansion strategy)
The full step-by-step playbook Grubhub used to launch new cities, covering supply acquisition, demand generation, and ongoing optimization

How it works: 1. Maintain city expansion spreadsheet to determine next target markets. As part of annual planning, figure out capacity for new markets and put them into the plan.
2. Once ready to launch, send 'paratrooper' salespeople into the best neighborhoods for two weeks. Get 50 restaurants to sign up. Pitch: Only pay when you receive an order, cancel anytime, no hidden fees.
3. Hire a local salesperson (early stage) OR staff an inside salesperson at HQ with the new territory (later stage) to continue growing supply.
4. Restaurants give you a few months to prove you can drive demand — this starts the clock for demand-side growth. First step: get restaurant pages live. Create SEO/SEM landing pages for every geo and cuisine combination (e.g., 'Nob Hill Thai delivery', '94107 food delivery').
5. Start bidding on Adwords for keywords where you have 4+ matching restaurants (decent conversion rate, can hit expected CAC targets based on 6-month payback period).
6. Email local bloggers in the market about the launch. Offer their readers $10 off first order via a market launch landing page.
7. After promo expires (30 days), redirect launch landing page to market SEO landing page to build page/domain authority.
8. Continue selling into the market. Drive conversion and retention improvements on demand side. Once hitting certain targets, open more demand-side growth efforts (e.g., transit advertising).
9. VP of Sales and growth lead review zip-code-level reports of supply and demand for ALL markets every month to focus teams on driving better liquidity in the right areas.

### High-Impact Product Surfaces for Demand-to-Supply Conversion (Demand driving supply: The little-understood growth loop behind a surprising number of iconic billion-dollar companies)
Prioritized list of product areas to optimize for converting demand-side users to supply-side users

How it works: In priority order based on traffic and contextual relevance: 1. Product listing/detail pages (highest traffic page type for most marketplaces) — add supply-side CTA and messaging. 2. Post-transaction/completion pages (e.g., post-purchase confirmation, survey completion) — show supply-side value prop and CTA when user has just had a positive demand-side experience. 3. Header/primary navigation — include both demand-side and supply-side CTAs (e.g., 'Find events' + 'Create an event'). Should be on all/most pages, above the fold. 4. In-product messaging flows (e.g., after messaging a host, show modal to list your home). 5. Expired/inactive content pages — repurpose high-traffic expired pages with supply-side CTAs. 6. Physical/offline touchpoints (e.g., printed tickets, receipts, packaging). 7. Targeted lifecycle emails triggered at the identified aha moment. For each surface, communicate three things: (1) Who you are (brand), (2) What you do (supply-side value), (3) CTA to take the supply-side action.

### Marketplace Supply Growth Lever Selection Checklist (How to Kickstart and Scale a Marketplace Business – Part 3: Cracking the Chicken-and-Egg Problem 🐣 - Growing Initial Supply)
A prioritized checklist for evaluating which supply growth levers to pursue, based on frequency of success across 17 major marketplaces

How it works: Instructions: Evaluate each lever for your marketplace. Remember the median company relied on just 2 levers — focus is critical.

□ 1. Direct sales (most common, ~60%) — Can you go door-to-door or make calls to recruit supply one-by-one? Do you know where your supply physically is?
□ 2. Referrals (~33%) — Can existing supply refer new supply? What incentive would motivate them? Can you create an ambassador program?
□ 3. Piggy-back existing network (~33%) — Is there an existing platform (Craigslist, Facebook groups, forums) where your potential supply already congregates?
□ 4. Word of mouth (~25%) — Is your supply's industry tight-knit? Will suppliers naturally tell peers?
□ 5. Subsidizing (~25%) — Can you guarantee income, subsidize equipment, or offer free leads to reduce supply's risk?
□ 6. Employees as supply — Can your team act as supply to create initial liquidity and learn the supply experience?
□ 7. Single-player mode — Can you build a tool that supply finds useful on its own, independent of demand?
□ 8. Performance marketing (rare for supply) — Are there search terms or targeting criteria that reach your supply effectively?
□ 9. Loops — Can demand-side users convert to supply? Can free users convert to paid?
□ 10. Events — Can you hold meetups, onboarding sessions, or attend industry events to recruit supply?
□ 11. SEO/Content (rare for supply) — Can you create content that attracts supply via search?
□ 12. Community leverage — Can passionate supply members form local chapters and evangelize on your behalf?

After evaluation: Pick your top 1-2 levers and go deep. Don't spread thin across many.

### Supply vs. Demand Focus Decision Checklist (How to Kickstart and Scale a Marketplace Business – Part 2: Cracking the Chicken-and-Egg Problem 🐣 - Supply vs. Demand)
A checklist to help marketplace founders determine whether to focus on supply or demand first.

How it works: Focus on SUPPLY first if:
☐ Your supply has its own built-in audience/customers (e.g., restaurants with loyal diners, event creators with attendees)
☐ Your supply IS your early demand (e.g., Etsy sellers were also buyers)
☐ Supply is hard to acquire (locked into competing platforms, high switching costs)
☐ More supply directly improves the demand experience (shorter wait times, more selection)

Focus on DEMAND first if:
☐ You offer super easy, super flexible, meaningful income — making supply acquisition trivially easy
☐ You have waitlists of potential supply providers
☐ Your initial supply is publicly available data you can repurpose
☐ You need demand to convince real supply to join (Zillow's public data → demand → real listings)

Reconsider if you're a marketplace at all if:
☐ Discovery/matching isn't core to your value prop
☐ Creators/suppliers bring 100% of their own demand
☐ You'd be better modeled as a platform/SaaS (like Shopify, WordPress)

## Examples

### Airbnb's City Launch Playbook for Supply (How to Kickstart and Scale a Marketplace Business – Part 3: Cracking the Chicken-and-Egg Problem 🐣 - Growing Initial Supply)
Airbnb's approach to launching in new cities by combining founder-led meetups with local team-driven direct sales targeting specific supply types

How it works: City Launch Steps:
1. Travel to the city and hold a founder-led meetup
   - In cities outside SF, meeting a founder is novel and exciting
   - Attendees would get excited and tell their friends (WOM amplification)
2. Deploy local teams accountable for their market
   - Teams decided which supply to acquire: which neighborhoods, what listing sizes, what price points
   - Critical for building the right mix of homes
3. Focus on immature markets without critical mass for direct sales effort
4. Religiously focus on making sure customers loved the experience

Supply loop addition:
- Person books trip → loves the experience → gets offer to make their home available for guests while on their trip (to help pay for it) → becomes a host

Key insight: Direct sales was critical especially in immature markets. Local team autonomy in supply selection was important for market-specific strategy.

### Breather Density Expansion Strategy (How to kickstart and scale a marketplace business)
Breather's precise approach to expanding supply within a single market by duplicating exactly what works.

How it works: Strategy: Ultra-precise duplication within a market before expanding.
- Had areas of density like Flatiron District where units kept getting booked immediately
- Decision framework questions:
  1. What is the exact same thing I can duplicate or extend?
  2. What else can this customer go do after?
  3. How can I double the size of the transaction?
